Linkages · how it fit together

Nature and people were connected

Whales and money. Early sketches of Manhattan show a whale in the Hudson River. A church charter claimed dead whales that washed ashore, for their oil and bone. Wildlife was part of the local economy.

One place, many services. Forest, wetland and shoreline gave food, clean water and protection from storms at the same time. Take one away and the others change too.

A different path. The ecologist wonders what if the country had been settled from the West: Manhattan might have been protected as a national park.

Explore · storm surge

Sea level is higher, then a storm pushes more water in

A storm surge is a wall of seawater pushed onto land by a hurricane or strong storm. When the sea is already higher, the same storm reaches farther inland.

Heavy rain can make it worse: rain falling on pavement has nowhere to soak in, so water pools in low streets and underground spaces.

Think about: which parts of a coastal city sit lowest, and what is underground there?

Data · what to check

Questions a planner puts on the map

Land
Low
Which blocks sit close to sea level?
People
Who?
Who lives there, and can they leave quickly?
Systems
Below
Which subway entrances and power rooms are below flood height?

These are the questions, not measured numbers. A real plan answers them with local maps and records.

Linkages · three parts at once

A resilient plan covers people, nature and machines

Social: warnings, evacuation help, neighbors checking on each other, who is most at risk.

Ecological: wetlands, oyster reefs, trees, parks and soil that soak up water. This is green infrastructure.

Technical: sea walls, pumps, flood gates, power and subways.

If one part fails, the others carry more of the load. That is why a plan needs all three.

Big idea 2 · planning with circularity

Cities and circularity

Many cities now plan their economies around circularity: reuse, repurpose and recirculate the materials we make and consume.

Examples to pictureReuse: a refillable bottle. Repurpose: an old building turned into homes. Recirculate: old materials made into new products.

Quick question: how is this different from a "take, make, throw away" city?

Tap to check.

A throw-away city sends materials one way, from the ground to a landfill. A circular city keeps them moving in loops, so it needs fewer new materials and makes less waste.

Big idea 2 · two ideas that work together

A sustainable and resilient city

Urban ecologyThe study of how living things, including people, relate to their urban environment.
ResilienceThe capacity of a city's systems to absorb disturbances, adapt to changing conditions and keep working.

Quick question: is a city with a great seawall automatically resilient?

Tap to check.

Not necessarily. A wall is only the technical part. If people cannot get warnings, or the wetlands behind it are gone, the whole system can still fail.

Ecology in, of and for cities

Ecology in the cityStudying nature that lives inside a city, like birds and plants in a park. This is the most basic kind.
Ecology of the cityStudying the whole city as a socio-ecological system, with people and nature together.
Ecology for the cityUsing what we learn to design and care for cities. This is stewardship, and it is the most complex.

18Big idea 1 · Types of urban ecology
Big idea 1 · ecology of the city

Urban metabolism

A city "eats" and "breathes" like a living body.

InWater, energy and materials flow into the city.
OutWastewater, waste, greenhouse gases and heat flow out.

Quick question: how could a city shrink what flows out?

Tap to check.

Use less going in (efficiency), and reuse what comes out: treat and reuse wastewater, recycle waste, and capture waste heat. This links back to circularity.

18Big idea 1 · Types of urban ecology
Big idea 1 · ecology of the city

Cities are "heterotrophic"

HeterotrophA living thing that cannot make its own food, so it must eat. Cities are like this: they consume much more energy than they produce.
Upside-down pyramidIn nature the producers at the base support the consumers. A city has huge numbers of consumers and few producers, so energy is imported.

Quick question: what would make a city less dependent on imports?

Tap to check.

Make more of its own energy, such as rooftop solar (the "renewable energy" arrow on the diagram), and use less in the first place.

Cities import most of their water

Water inAbout two-thirds (67%) is imported. Only 33% is local groundwater.
But where does it go?About 39% leaves as wastewater and only 4% as measured stormwater. The biggest slice, 57%, is "residual" on the chart.

Quick question: what could "residual" water be?

Tap to check.

It is the part not counted as discharge. Our reading: water used up or kept in the city, such as evaporation from lawns, water in plants, and water that soaks into the ground. Check the source for the exact meaning.

Takeaway

A city is an ecosystem. Design it with people, nature and technology together, and it can bounce back.

  1. Study the city in three ways: nature in it, the whole city as a system, and design for it.
  2. Compact, circular cities use fewer resources than sprawling ones.
  3. A resilient city plans for the social, ecological and technical parts together.
