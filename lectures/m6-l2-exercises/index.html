Linkages · how it fit together

Nature and people were connected

Whales and money. Early sketches of Manhattan show a whale in the Hudson River. A church charter claimed dead whales that washed ashore, for their oil and bone. Wildlife was part of the local economy.

One place, many services. Forest, wetland and shoreline gave food, clean water and protection from storms at the same time. Take one away and the others change too.

A different path. The ecologist wonders what if the country had been settled from the West: Manhattan might have been protected as a national park.

Explore · storm surge

Sea level is higher, then a storm pushes more water in

A storm surge is a wall of seawater pushed onto land by a hurricane or strong storm. When the sea is already higher, the same storm reaches farther inland.

Heavy rain can make it worse: rain falling on pavement has nowhere to soak in, so water pools in low streets and underground spaces.

Think about: which parts of a coastal city sit lowest, and what is underground there?

Data · what to check

Questions a planner puts on the map

Land
Low
Which blocks sit close to sea level?
People
Who?
Who lives there, and can they leave quickly?
Systems
Below
Which subway entrances and power rooms are below flood height?

These are the questions, not measured numbers. A real plan answers them with local maps and records.

Linkages · three parts at once

A resilient plan covers people, nature and machines

Social: warnings, evacuation help, neighbors checking on each other, who is most at risk.

Ecological: wetlands, oyster reefs, trees, parks and soil that soak up water. This is green infrastructure.

Technical: sea walls, pumps, flood gates, power and subways.

If one part fails, the others carry more of the load. That is why a plan needs all three.
