Trade-off

1.3Externalities
Costs and benefits that spill over

Who is left out of the deal?

Sellera factory makes and sells a product
⇄
Buyerpays the price for the product
Bystandersneighbours breathe the smoke, but nobody pays them and nobody asked them
Negative externality

A cost pushed onto others. Example: runoff from farm fertilizer feeds algae blooms in a lake downstream.

Positive externality

A benefit others get for free. Example: a neighbour's beehives pollinate your garden.

Return to the warm-up poll: the town downstream is the bystander. When the polluter does not pay, the price of the product is "too low" because it hides a cost. Policy tools that make polluters pay (taxes, fines, permits) are ways of "internalising" the externality. Keep this short; the game on the next slide does the practice.
1.3Sorting game
Game · positive or negative?

Sort the externalities

Positive: a free benefit to people outside the deal. Negative: an unpaid cost to people outside the deal.

Truck traffic noise next to an elementary school
Fertilizer runoff causes an algae bloom downstream
A restored wetland upstream reduces flooding in towns below
Street trees planted by one homeowner shade and cool the sidewalk

Give pairs 3 minutes. Debrief the carbon dioxide row: it is the biggest negative externality of all because the "bystanders" include people in other countries and future generations. The street tree row links to Phoenix heat: shade is a benefit that spills over to everyone walking by.
1.3The commons
Garrett Hardin, 1968

The Tragedy of the Commons

A commons is a resource that many people can use and no one person controls: a shared pasture, a fishery, groundwater, the air. Picture herders sharing one pasture. Should I add one more cow?

My gain
+1

I get the whole benefit of the extra cow: more milk, more money.

The cost of overgrazing
÷ all

The damage to the grass is shared by every herder. My share of the cost is small.

When everyone makes the same "smart" choice, the pasture is ruined for all.

Hardin's 1968 essay in Science made the idea famous. Note that the tragedy also works like an externality: each herder pushes part of the cost of their cow onto the others. Ask for other commons students use: the Colorado River, campus Wi-Fi bandwidth, a shared kitchen fridge. Next slide: students play the fishery version.
1.3Simulation
Simulation · 20 fishing seasons on a shared stock

Can the fishery last?

Each boat tries to catch 10 tonnes per season.

A teaching model: the fish regrow fastest when the stock is about half full, and cannot regrow once it is nearly gone.

Round 1 (3 min): no catch limit. Ask pairs to find the number of boats that gives the biggest total catch over 20 seasons. Round 2 (3 min): turn on the community limit and try 20 or 30 boats again. Then submit. Debrief with the admin page: 15 boats (150 t a season) is the most the stock can supply forever. At 16 to 17 boats the 20-season total looks bigger, but the stock is shrinking and overfished; at 18 or more boats it collapses and the total catch falls. With the limit on, any fleet size keeps the stock healthy (2,800 t). Ask: why would one captain still want to add a boat? That is the tragedy. Point out the trade-off at 0 boats: a healthy stock, but no food or jobs. Model: logistic growth with r = 0.6 and a carrying capacity of 1,000 t, starting at 800 t; the largest catch it can sustain is 150 t per season.
1.3Data analysis
Data · a real Tragedy of the Commons

The collapse of Newfoundland's cod

Northern cod catch off Newfoundland, Canada (tonnes per year)

Approximate figures. In 1992 Canada closed the fishery, and more than 30,000 people lost fishing and fish-plant jobs.

Your turn

1. Factory trawlers arrived in the 1950s and 60s. How many times bigger was the 1968 catch than the best years before them?

2. The 2024 catch limit is what share of the 1968 peak?

Figures are rounded. Source: Fisheries and Oceans Canada (DFO) northern cod (NAFO Divisions 2J3KL) stock assessments, summarised in Hutchings and Myers (1994) and in the Wikipedia article "Collapse of the Atlantic northwest cod fishery". Catch peaked at about 810,000 t in 1968, roughly three times the best years before factory trawlers. The moratorium was announced on July 2, 1992. In June 2024 Canada ended the moratorium and set a commercial limit of 18,000 t. Answers: about 3 times; about 2%. Discussion: the cod were a commons shared by many fleets, including foreign ones before Canada's 200-mile limit in 1977. Who paid the cost? Mostly small coastal communities, which links to environmental justice.
1.3Collective action
Is the tragedy unavoidable?

Communities can protect a commons

Elinor Ostrom studied fisheries, forests and water systems around the world that people had managed well for generations. In 2009 she became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in economics.

What successful groups do
  • Agree on who can use the resource
  • Let users help make the rules
  • Watch how much is taken
  • Apply fair, step-by-step penalties
Examples
  • Farmers agreeing on groundwater pumping limits
  • Arizona's 1980 Groundwater Management Act
  • The 1987 Montreal Protocol on ozone
  • Your community catch limit in the simulation
Ostrom's work, especially "Governing the Commons" (1990), showed Hardin's tragedy is not inevitable. Her list has eight design principles; four are shown in plain words. Collective action can be local (a farmers' cooperative), state-level (Arizona's Groundwater Management Act created Active Management Areas such as Phoenix) or global (Montreal Protocol). Ask: which of the four features did the simulation's catch limit have, and which was missing? (It had a rule, but no monitoring or penalties.)
1.3Environmental justice
Discussion · think, pair, share

Who carries the burden?

Scenario: the city needs a new waste transfer station. The cheapest land is next to a low-income neighbourhood that already has a freeway and two factories.

BurdensWho pays the costs?

Who gets the truck traffic, smell and noise? Is it the same group every time?

BenefitsWho gains?

Who gets lower trash fees and cleaner streets? Do they live near the station?

VoiceWho decides?

Were the neighbours asked? How could the city make the process fairer?

Background: in 1982, residents of Warren County, North Carolina, a mostly Black community, protested a toxic waste landfill. Their protest helped start the environmental justice movement.

Think for 1 minute, pair for 3, share for 4. Push students to use the other terms: the station creates negative externalities; siting is a trade-off; neighbourhood groups organising is collective action. Background: the 1982 Warren County PCB landfill protests; the 1987 United Church of Christ report "Toxic Wastes and Race in the United States" documented the pattern nationally. Phoenix link: studies of the Valley find that lower-income neighbourhoods tend to have less tree shade and are hotter in summer.

    Three things to remember
    1. Externalities are costs or benefits that land on people outside a deal. Negative ones are often paid by those with the least power.
    2. Shared resources get overused when each user gains alone but the damage is shared: the Tragedy of the Commons.
    3. Collective action, like a fair catch limit, can protect a commons. Environmental justice asks who decides and who pays.
