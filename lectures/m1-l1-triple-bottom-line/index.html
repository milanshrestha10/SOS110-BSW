1.1Sustainability
Term 1 · Sustainability

What does sustainable mean?

“Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.”

Our Common Future (the Brundtland Report), United Nations, 1987

Today

People alive now need food, water, energy, homes and jobs.

Limits

Nature can only give so much. Use resources no faster than they recover.

Tomorrow

Leave future generations the same chance to meet their needs.

This is the most quoted definition of sustainable development, from the UN World Commission on Environment and Development (chaired by Gro Harlem Brundtland), 1987. Link to the glossary example: harvesting timber no faster than the forest regrows. Ask: what is one thing you use every day that might not be available to your grandchildren?
1.1Triple bottom line
Term 2 · Triple Bottom Line (TBL)

Three ways to measure success

People
Planet
Prosperity
Bearable Equitable Viable
Sustainable
People · equity

Are workers and neighbours healthy, safe and treated fairly?

Planet · environment

Does it protect air, water, land and wildlife, and cut waste?

Prosperity · economy

Can it pay its way, create jobs and last over time?

A normal "bottom line" counts only money. The triple bottom line counts all three.

John Elkington coined "triple bottom line" in 1994. "Bottom line" is the last line of a company's accounts: profit or loss. The overlap labels (bearable, equitable, viable) are a common way to draw the idea: an action that only helps two pillars is not yet sustainable. Glossary example: packaging that cuts plastic, fair wages, and still a profit.

Give 2 minutes, then check. The follow-up is the real lesson: which items help more than one pillar? The wetland also filters water for people; lower factory costs could come from saving energy (planet); a living wage also keeps staff and saves hiring costs (prosperity). Real decisions rarely sit in one box.
1.1Simulation
Simulation · Town budget

Spend $10 million on a desert town

Budget used$10.0M of $10M
Balance score
0
out of 100 · set by your weakest pillar

A teaching model, not real data.

Pairs build a plan for a Phoenix-area town, then submit it. The balance score is the lowest of the three pillar scores, so pouring all the money into one favourite program always scores badly. Each program has diminishing returns, so spreading money helps. The starting split ($2.5M each) scores 78; the best possible is 79 (for example $3M solar, $2M cooling, $2.5M bus, $2.5M refund), so a perfect score is impossible: that is the trade-off. Debrief with the admin page: which pillar was weakest most often? Would a real town weigh the pillars equally? Who decides?
1.1The SDGs
Term 3 · Sustainable Development Goals

17 goals, one shared roadmap

Adopted by all 193 UN member states in 2015, with targets for 2030. With a neighbour: which goals are mainly about Planet, People or Prosperity?

The UN adopted the 2030 Agenda and its 17 SDGs in September 2015. The grouping revealed by the button is the "SDG wedding cake" from the Stockholm Resilience Centre (Rockström and Sukhdev, 2016): the biosphere goals (6, 13, 14, 15) are the base, society sits on top of them, the economy on top of society, and Goal 17 (partnerships) ties them together. Point out that this matches People, Planet, Prosperity, but with the planet as the foundation. Glossary example: a city upgrading its water system supports Goal 6.

Source: United Nations, World Population Prospects (UN DESA). The 2024 revision projects about 9.7 billion in 2050, peaking around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s. Ask: how many years did it take to add each billion? (About 14, 13, 12, 12, 11.) The world passed 5 billion the same year the Brundtland Report came out. What does "meeting the needs of the present" mean when the present keeps getting bigger? Which pillar feels this most?
1.1Wicked problems
Term 4 · Wicked Problems · discuss in pairs

Wicked problem or tame problem?

Signs of a wicked problem
  • No single agreed definition
  • No clear point where it is "solved"
  • Answers are better or worse, not right or wrong
  • Every fix changes the problem
  • People disagree on what matters
Fixing a leaking water pipeA clear goal and a clear finish: the leak stops.
Global climate changeEnergy, money, fairness and politics in every country at once.
Extreme summer heat in PhoenixHealth, housing, energy costs and city design all interact.
Building a bus schedule for one routeHard work, but experts can agree on a good answer.
Sharing Colorado River waterMany states, tribes, farms and cities want the same water.
The term comes from Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber (1973), who studied planning problems. After checking, invite disagreement: could a bus schedule become wicked if the route cuts through a neighbourhood that does not want it? That is the point: most sustainability problems are wicked, so the triple bottom line helps us weigh them, but it does not "solve" them. Preview: systems thinking (next readings) is a tool for wicked problems.
1.1Exit ticket
Exit ticket · counts for participation

A company cuts its plastic waste, pays fair wages, and still makes a profit. Which idea does this show best?

Correct answer: B. The company succeeds on Planet (less plastic), People (fair wages) and Prosperity (still profitable). This is the glossary example for the Triple Bottom Line.

    Three things to remember
    1. Sustainability means meeting today's needs without taking away the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
    2. The triple bottom line judges success by People, Planet and Prosperity together, not by money alone.
    3. The 17 SDGs give the world a shared roadmap, but most sustainability challenges are wicked problems with no single right answer.
