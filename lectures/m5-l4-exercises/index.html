Benefits · bridge fuel

Gas can bridge from coal to clean energy

A bridge fuel helps us get from one energy source to a better one. Here is the case for gas.

Burns cleaner than coal
About half
the CO₂ of coal for the same electricity, and far less soot and sulfur.
Fills the gaps
On demand
Gas plants can switch on fast when the sun sets or the wind drops.
Jobs and income
Local
Drilling pays workers, landowners and local governments.
Costs · what goes wrong

Gas has costs that do not show up on a bill

Getting gas out of the ground and to a stove or power plant leaves a mess behind.

Leaked methane
80× CO₂
Over 20 years, methane traps more than 80 times the heat of the same weight of CO₂. Leaks add up.
Water
Wastewater
Fracking uses a lot of water, and the used water must be cleaned or injected deep underground. Some injection wells have been linked to small earthquakes.
Neighbors
Air and noise
Wells, trucks and flares sit close to homes in some places.
Key idea · externality

An externality is a cost someone else pays

An externality is a cost (or benefit) that lands on people who were not part of the deal. The price of gas does not include it.

Example: the buyer pays for the gas and the seller is paid. A family downwind breathes the fumes, and the whole planet gets the leaked methane. Nobody charges the seller or buyer for that.

Key idea · wicked problem

A wicked problem has no single right fix

A wicked problem is tangled up with many people and many goals. Every fix changes something else, and people disagree on what “solved” means.

Gas fits: it solves one problem (dirty coal power) and creates another (methane leaks, water use, new fossil fuel pipes that last for decades).

Three questions for your group

1Who pays the costs that are not in the price of gas?

2How does gas solve one problem and create another?

3Is gas a bridge to clean energy, or a place we get stuck? Why?

Step 2 · Key variables

Show what builds up and what flows

A stock is an amount that builds up, like water in a tub. An inflow adds to it. An outflow drains it. Draw each stock as a box, and each flow as a thick arrow.

Stock
A box
Something that piles up. Ask: what builds up in this story?
Inflow
Arrow in
What fills the stock. Ask: what adds to it?
Outflow
Arrow out
What drains the stock. Ask: what takes it away?

Ideas to start with: wells drilled, gas in the market, gas price, wastewater stored, contaminated water, people exposed, cleanup cost.

Step 3 · Feedback

Find the loops that speed up and slow down

A feedback loop is a circle of causes. Label each arrow + if both things move the same way, or − if they move opposite ways.

R · Reinforcing
Speeds up
A positive loop. A change feeds more of the same change.
B · Balancing
Slows down
A negative loop. A change triggers a push back.
Your job
Find one of each
Draw at least one R loop and one B loop for natural gas.
Step 4 · Leverage points

Where would a small change help the most?

A leverage point is a spot where a small push changes a lot. Circle two or three on your diagram, and write the change next to each. For example: a rule, a price, a tool, or a habit.

Ask: which change would cut the harm to people who did not choose it (the negative externality)? It is fine if it only reduces the harm and does not solve it. Wicked problems rarely get solved.
