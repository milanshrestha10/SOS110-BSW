Nepal: a cascade of hazards

A cascading risk is a chain where one hazard sets off the next. Follow the Bhotekoshi–Trishuli floods from left to right. Tap each red number.

Step 1 · Warming earth

Warmer air heats the high mountains

Over many years, warmer air heats mountain peaks and slopes. Nothing breaks yet. But every step after this one becomes more likely.

Step 2 · Thawing ground and glaciers

Ice that held the slope together lets go

Permafrost is ground that stays frozen all year. Frozen water works like glue between rock pieces. When the ground thaws, the glue weakens and bedrock and slopes become unstable.

A hanging glacier is a glacier perched on a steep slope, high above the valley. Melting makes it crack and loosen.

Step 3 · Collapse

A rock-ice avalanche falls

A huge mass of ice and rock breaks loose at once and crashes down the slope. This is an ice-rock avalanche. It can happen with no storm and little warning.

Step 4 · Pulverized rock and water

The fall turns into a slurry

The fall grinds rock into gravel and dust, and melts ice. This mixes with river water and meltwater. Debris can also block the river and form a lake that later bursts.

Step 5 · Debris flow

Steep slopes make it fast and heavy

A debris flow is a fast river of water, mud, gravel and boulders. A gradient is how steep a slope is. On a high gradient, the flow speeds up and can carry huge boulders for many miles.

Step 6 · Downstream communities

Flood risk no one can plan for

Villages, roads, bridges and power plants sit on flat land by the river, right in the path. The water can arrive with little warning, far from where it started.

The story

How one risk leads to the next

1. Hydraulic fracturing (fracking) starts it. Crews pump water, sand and chemicals deep underground at high pressure. The rock cracks, and gas flows out of the cracks.

2. Wastewater comes back up. Each well returns a lot of used water that can be salty and carry chemicals from the rock. It has to be stored, trucked, treated or injected deep underground.

3. Something leaks or spills. A weak well casing, a tank, a pit or a truck accident can let fluid escape. Most wells do not fail, but there are many wells.

4. Water becomes contaminated. Fluid that escapes can reach groundwater or a river. Water moves, so the problem can spread far from the well.

5. Drinking water is affected. Families with private wells and towns that draw from the river or aquifer may be drinking it. The 2016 US EPA study found fracking can affect drinking water in some cases.

6. Public health is at risk. Chemicals in drinking water can cause illness, and people worry even when the risk is unclear. Scientists are still studying how big the health effects are.

7. Cleanup, costs and rules follow. Someone pays for testing, cleanup and bottled water, and communities lose trust. Neighbors and taxpayers often pay, not the company. That is the externality.

Benefits · bridge fuel

Gas can bridge from coal to clean energy

A bridge fuel helps us get from one energy source to a better one. Here is the case for gas.

Costs · what goes wrong

Gas has costs that do not show up on a bill

Getting gas out of the ground and to a stove or power plant leaves a mess behind.

Leaked methane
80× CO₂
Over 20 years, methane traps more than 80 times the heat of the same weight of CO₂. Leaks add up.
Water
Wastewater
Fracking uses a lot of water, and the used water must be cleaned or injected deep underground. Some injection wells have been linked to small earthquakes.
Neighbors
Air and noise
Wells, trucks and flares sit close to homes in some places.
Key idea · externality

An externality is a cost someone else pays

An externality is a cost (or benefit) that lands on people who were not part of the deal. The price of gas does not include it.

Example: the buyer pays for the gas and the seller is paid. A family downwind breathes the fumes, and the whole planet gets the leaked methane. Nobody charges the seller or buyer for that.

Key idea · wicked problem

A wicked problem has no single right fix

A wicked problem is tangled up with many people and many goals. Every fix changes something else, and people disagree on what “solved” means.

Gas fits: it solves one problem (dirty coal power) and creates another (methane leaks, water use, new fossil fuel pipes that last for decades).

Step 4 · Leverage points

Where would a small change help the most?

A leverage point is a spot where a small push changes a lot. Circle two or three on your diagram, and write the change next to each. For example: a rule, a price, a tool, or a habit.

Ask: which change would cut the harm to people who did not choose it (the negative externality)? It is fine if it only reduces the harm and does not solve it. Wicked problems rarely get solved.
