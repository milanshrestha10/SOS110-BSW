12Big idea 1 · Systems
Big idea 1 · how do systems work?

Systems can be open or closed

A system is a set of parts that work together to produce something no single part could do alone. What crosses its boundary decides whether it is open or closed.

Energy and matter both cross the boundary. Most real ecosystems are open.

Only energy crosses the boundary. Earth is open for energy but closed for matter: nothing disappears.

12Big idea 1 · Stocks
Big idea 1 · the bathtub model

A stock changes only through its flows

A stock is an amount we can observe and measure over time, like the water in a tub. Flows are the rates that fill it (inflows) or drain it (outflows).

Figures: Sherman & Montgomery (2023), Fig. 10.4. The clouds mean "outside the system."

12Big idea 1 · Feedback
Big idea 1 · feedback loops

Feedback loops amplify or dampen change

Positive feedback · reinforcing

A change causes more of the same change. It speeds up and pushes the system away from balance.

Negative feedback · balancing

A change triggers a response that pulls the system back toward balance. It dampens change.

+Same direction: more leads to more, less to less−Opposite direction: more leads to lessCount the − links: an even number makes a reinforcing loop, an odd number a balancing loop.

Big idea 1 · how can we show how a system works?

Two diagrams, one system

StockWhat accumulates over time. Here, the number of people alive.
Flow · inflowThe movement in. Births add to the stock, like water from a faucet.
The key ideaA stock changes only when inflow ≠ outflow. More births than deaths, and the population grows.
Causal loopMaps cause → effect. A + means both move the same way; a − means opposite ways.
R · ReinforcingMore people → more births → more people. It amplifies change (runaway growth).
B · BalancingMore people → more deaths → fewer people. It restores balance (stabilizes).

12Big idea 1 · Change points
Big idea 1 · what happens when a system changes

Thresholds, tipping points and leverage points

Threshold

The system shifts

A threshold is a point at which a system shifts significantly. Past it, the system settles into a new state.

Tipping point

Hard to predict

Tipping points are difficult to predict: complex interactions make the exact threshold uncertain.

Leverage point

Small change, big effect

A leverage point is a place in a system where a small change can produce large effects, like national energy-efficiency standards.

Why it mattersA reinforcing loop can push a system past a threshold, like nutrient runoff tipping coastal water into a dead zone. A leverage point is where a small action can steer it back.

12Big idea 2 · Cycles
Big idea 2 · natural cycles for water, oxygen, nitrogen, carbon and phosphorus

Energy flows through, matter cycles around

Earth is an open system for energy

Photosynthesis

The engine for life. Plants capture sunlight and clean the air of CO₂.

Slow geological cycles

Volcanic eruptions and weathering release elements from rock.

Earth is a closed system for matter

Nothing disappears

The same atoms cycle through air, water, rock and living things.

Ecosystem energetics

Energy moves up food webs and energy pyramids, with feedback loops along the way.

12Big idea 2 · Cycles
Big idea 2 · how life-support systems stay in balance

Biogeochemical cycles

A biogeochemical cycle is the complete path of one element through Earth's systems.

  • Bio: where the element is inside living things (the biosphere)
  • Geo: where it is in non-living parts of Earth: air, water and rock
  • Matter stays in each stock for very different lengths of time, from days in the air to millions of years in rock

The oxygen cycle

  1. In: O₂ enters the air through photosynthesis.
  2. Out: carbon returns to the air as CO₂ through respiration and burning.
  3. Matter cycles between the lithosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere.

12Big idea 2 · Biosphere 2
Big idea 2 · an experiment in the Arizona desert

Biosphere 2: a sealed world ran short of oxygen

In 1991, eight people sealed themselves inside for two years with a rain forest, ocean, desert and farm.

  1. Rich soil fed microbes that breathed out CO₂ and used up O₂.
  2. Glass cut the sunlight, so plants made less O₂.
  3. The concrete walls soaked up CO₂, locking the oxygen away.

12Big idea 2 · Human impacts
Big idea 2 · human-induced changes to natural cycles

Too much of a good thing

Humans and the phosphorus cycle
  • We mine phosphate rock (apatite) for fertilizer, detergents and steel.
  • The phosphorus washing into the oceans each year has tripled.
  • In large doses it changes lakes, rivers and seas through eutrophication.
Humans and the nitrogen cycle
  • Plants need nitrogen to grow. Legumes like beans fix their own from the air.
  • Farms add huge amounts of synthetic nitrogen fertilizer.
  • Crops take up only about half. The rest is lost to water and air.
  • Nitrate is a leading drinking water contaminant.
Systems viewFertilizer speeds up the inflow to soil faster than crops can use it. The extra flows on, into rivers, groundwater and the sea.
12Big idea 3 · Eutrophication
Big idea 3 · the eutrophication conundrum

Eutrophication: when nutrients suffocate water

Eutrophication is when too many nutrients in a lake, river or sea cause a fast growth, death and decay of algae. Decay uses up the oxygen that fish and other animals need.

1Fertilizer and sewage wash into water
2Algae grow fast: a bloom
3Algae die and sink
4Bacteria decompose them and use up oxygen
5Fish and shrimp leave or die
6A dead zone forms
12Big idea 3 · Dead zone
Big idea 3 · the Gulf of America (formerly the Gulf of Mexico)

A dead zone appears every summer

  1. The Mississippi drains about 40% of the lower 48 states, including the Corn Belt.
  2. Each spring, it carries fertilizer runoff from farms and runoff from cities into the Gulf.
  3. By summer, bottom water off Louisiana drops below 2 mg/L of oxygen: too little for fish and shrimp.

Map: N. Rabalais, LSU/LUMCON/NOAA, in Sherman & Montgomery (2023), Fig. 10.17.

The iceberg model, Fig. 10.24. The textbook uses the Gulf's former name.

12Big idea 3 · Tres Rios

Tres Rios: a wetland that cleans water

The Tres Rios constructed treatment wetland. Marsh plants and microbes take up nitrogen and phosphorus left in treated wastewater.

A conventional treatment plant. Sophisticated, expensive and energy-intensive tanks and pumps do the same job.

Tres Rios means "three rivers": the Salt, Gila and Agua Fria meet near the wetland, in the southwest Valley.

The takeaway

Earth keeps its matter. When we speed up one flow, a stock somewhere else fills up.

  1. A stock rises or falls only when its inflows and outflows differ.
  2. Reinforcing loops speed change up; balancing loops pull it back.
  3. Cutting nutrient runoff, on farms and in cities, shrinks the dead zone.
