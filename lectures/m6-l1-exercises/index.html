Stage 1 · The textbook infographic, step 1

From fiber to fabric

Jeans are usually cotton plus a stretchy fiber called elastane. Elastane is made from petroleum-based plastics.

Farming uses pesticides and chemical fertilizers. Some fiber is wasted when cotton is spun into thread, and dyeing puts chemicals into wastewater.

Stage 2 · Typical, not from the figure

Cutting and sewing

Fabric is cut into pieces and sewn together. Scraps of cloth are left over. Washing and dyeing the fabric use a lot of water and chemicals.

This step is a typical description. It is not from the textbook’s step 1 infographic.

Stage 3 · Typical, not from the figure

Shipping and selling

Jeans are packed, shipped and stored. Packaging and tags are added. Jeans that do not sell can end up as waste.

Typical description. Not from the textbook figure.

Stage 4 · Typical, not from the figure

Wearing and washing

Most of the time you own jeans is spent wearing and washing them. Each wash uses water and energy. How long you keep them changes the whole life cycle.

Typical description. Not from the textbook figure.

Stage 5 · You decide

The end of the line

One day the jeans are done for you. They can be handed down, resold, turned into something new, or thrown away. Slide 4 is about this step.

SOS110 · Class Exercise 17 · Exercise 1 · Life cycle of jeans

Where do the jeans go?

Five choices for the end of the line. The best ones come first. Tap an icon, then tap the pencil to answer.

Exercise17This slideEnd of lifeChoices follow the order of reduce, reuse, recycle, dispose from chapter 15. Descriptions are typical, not from the figure.
End of life

Wear them longer

Every extra year you wear them is a year with no new jeans made. Nothing is wasted at stages 1 to 3.

End of life

Repair them

A patch or a new button keeps the jeans in use. This slows the loop, which is one of the three circular economy strategies.

End of life

Resell or hand them down

Reuse keeps jeans out of the landfill and replaces a new pair. Resale and swaps are growing ways to do it.

End of life

Recycle the fabric

Old fabric can be shredded and made into a different product. That is usually open-loop recycling: the new product is lower quality, so it works as downcycling.

End of life

Throw them away

Landfilled jeans take up space and are never used again. Burned jeans make some heat but release carbon dioxide and other pollution. Everything made upstream is lost.

ASU Zero Waste

Your room has two bins

ASU’s zero waste checklist says your room should have a deskside recycling bin and a deskside landfill bin. If either is missing, you can send a maintenance request. Find out where to empty each one.

ASU Zero Waste

What goes in the blue bin

  • Yes: paper you can rip, cardboard, rigid plastics like bottles, jugs and tubs, and metal cans and foil.
  • No: plastic bags and other soft plastic, Styrofoam, and anything smaller than 2.5 inches.
  • Tips: empty liquids first and keep food out. A pizza box counts only with no grease and no pizza.

ASU’s lists have changed over time. Check the ASU Waste Directory.

ASU Zero Waste

Soft plastic and e-waste

ASU runs separate collections for soft plastics (film and bags) and for e-waste (old electronics). They do not go in the blue bin. Use the ASU Waste Directory to find where to take them.

We could not verify drop-off spots for individual residence halls.

ASU Zero Waste

Food scraps

ASU composts food waste and compostable paper in its dining halls and some other places. The residence hall compost program is paused for now, so scraps from your room go in the landfill bin unless you take them to a dining hall bin. Check the ASU Zero Waste pages for what is open today.

ASU Zero Waste

Make less in the first place

ASU’s checklist suggests: split shared supplies with your roommates, buy durable things that last past one year, rent big appliances, and swap single-use items for reusable bags, containers, utensils and water bottles.

SOS110 · Class Exercise 17 · Takeaway

Close the loop

Every product has a life cycle. Every bin is a choice.

  1. A pair of jeans makes waste long before you buy it.
  2. Wearing, repairing and reusing beat recycling, and recycling beats the landfill.
  3. Sorting well in your residence hall keeps the blue bin clean and the landfill small.
