3.1Big ideas
Big ideas

How many people can Earth support?

It depends on how fast we grow, where we live, and how much each of us uses from Earth's life-support systems.

1

Growth has limits

Every population meets a carrying capacity. Overshooting it can damage the capacity itself.

2

Booms are temporary

Deaths fall first and births follow later. The gap between them is the population boom, and it closes.

3

Consumption counts

Our impact is people times consumption times technology, so the number of people is only part of the story.

Frame the lecture with the question on the slide. Ask for a show of hands: too many people, or too much consumption? Come back to the same question on the I = PAT slide.

3.1Population ecology
What population ecology teaches us

J-curves, S-curves and overshoot

Click through the four tabs. Exponential: growth in proportion to size, the J-curve (bacteria in a dish, reindeer on St. Matthew Island at first). Logistic: growth slows as the population nears carrying capacity K and levels off in an S-curve. Overshoot: when feedback is delayed the population shoots past K and then falls back. Degradation: if the overshoot damages the resource base (overgrazed grass, eroded soil, depleted aquifers), K itself falls and the population crashes to a lower level. Curves are a model, not data.
3.1Malthus
Thomas Robert Malthus (1766–1834)

The Malthusian trap

Thomas Robert Malthus
1766–1834

"Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio." (An Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798)

Left: Malthus's two ratios. Right: for most of history, extra food became extra people, not richer people, so income per person stayed flat. After about 1800, farming and industrial technology, then the demographic transition, broke the trap. Both curves are sketches, not data.

Malthus argued that population always catches up with food, so famine, disease and war ("positive checks") keep most people poor. That held for thousands of years: income per person barely moved before 1800. He missed two things: technology that raised food output far faster than arithmetic (the Agricultural and Industrial Revolutions, later the Green Revolution), and the demographic transition, where people choose smaller families as they get richer. Ask: is he still wrong if we count the ecological footprint and not just food? That is the bridge to I = PAT.

3.1The model
Demographic Transition Model

Deaths fall first, births follow later

Birth rate (CBR)Babies born each year per 1,000 people.
Death rate (CDR)Deaths each year per 1,000 people.
Natural increaseBirth rate minus death rate. 20 per 1,000 is 2% growth a year.
Total fertility (TFR)Children per woman over her life. About 2.1 replaces the parents.
Life expectancyYears a newborn can expect to live at today's death rates.

After Max Roser (2019), "Demographic transition: Why is rapid population growth a temporary phenomenon?", Our World in Data. Curves are a sketch, not data.

Walk through the stages left to right. Stage 1: high births and high deaths, little growth (Sweden before 1800). Stage 2: better food, clean water and medicine cut deaths, but families stay large, so the population booms (Niger today). Stage 3: as child deaths fall, schooling spreads and people move to cities, families get smaller (Kenya). Stage 4: both rates low, little growth (United States). Stage 5: deaths exceed births (Japan, China). Roser's key point: if fertility had fallen at the same time as mortality, there would have been no population boom at all; the boom is the gap between the two lines.
3.1I = PAT
Measuring our impact

I = PAT and the ecological footprint

IImpact=PPopulation×AAffluence×TTechnology
I · CO₂ a year

Ecological footprint

The land and sea area needed to grow our food, fiber and timber, hold our buildings and roads, and absorb our carbon, measured in global hectares.

Earth has about 1.6 global hectares per person. The world uses about 1.7 Earths a year: that is overshoot.

Earths needed if everyone lived like the average person in each country. Rounded from the Global Footprint Network (National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts).

I = PAT (Ehrlich and Holdren, 1971) says impact is population times affluence (consumption per person) times technology (impact per unit of consumption). The "World today" preset uses CO₂ as the impact: about 8.1 billion people × about $17,000 of output per person × about 0.27 tonnes of CO₂ per $1,000 gives roughly 37 billion tonnes, close to world fossil CO₂ emissions. Try the presets: 25% fewer people cuts impact by 25%, but doubling consumption doubles it, and cleaner technology can cut it more than either. The ecological footprint turns all of this into area: the average American's footprint would need about 5 Earths if everyone lived that way; the average Kenyan's, well under one. All numbers are rounded teaching numbers; please check the footprint values against the latest Global Footprint Network data.
