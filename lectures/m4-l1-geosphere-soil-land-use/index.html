11Human impacts
Human impacts on Earth's surface · three main categories

Three ways we change ecosystems' structure and function

Soil loss

Agriculture, monoculture in particular, strips and exhausts the soil surface.

Structure
Topsoil thins; fewer roots, fungi and soil animals
Function
Less water held, fewer nutrients cycled, lower yields

Mining

Digging up metals and minerals exposes soil and contaminates the surface.

Structure
Landforms removed; habitat replaced by pits and waste rock
Function
Metals leach into water; the land stops growing plants

Land use and urbanization

Converting land changes ecosystems and Earth's biogeochemical cycles.

Structure
Desert, forest or farmland becomes pavement and roofs
Function
Rain runs off instead of soaking in; cities store heat
Structure is what an ecosystem is made of (soil layers, plants, animals, landforms). Function is what it does (holds and filters water, cycles nutrients, stores carbon, grows food). Each human impact changes both. Ask students to name a Phoenix example of each: farmland soil in the West Valley, copper mines in eastern Arizona, and new subdivisions on the desert edge.

11Big idea 1 · Geosphere
Big idea 1 · how the geosphere works

The ground beneath our feet

The geosphere is the solid Earth: crust, mantle and core. The lithosphere is its rigid outer shell (the crust plus the top of the mantle), broken into tectonic plates that ride on hotter, softer rock below.

It makes the planet habitable by

  • Providing a physical habitat for land plants and animals
  • Supplying essential nutrients for plant growth
  • Regulating the carbon cycle through weathering and other geological processes
Walk down the diagram from the thin crust to the core. The crust is thinnest under the oceans and thickest under mountains. Weathering of rock slowly pulls carbon dioxide out of the air, which is one way the geosphere regulates climate over long time scales. The diagram credit (ScienceFacts.net) is printed on the image.
11Big idea 1 · Soil
Big idea 1 · where rock meets life

Soil is a slow-made resource

Soil forms when weathered rock mixes with organic matter, water, air and living things.

  • Topsoil (A): dark, rich in organic matter and life; where most roots grow
  • Subsoil (B): collects clay and minerals washed down from above
  • Weathered bedrock (C): broken rock slowly turning into soil
Why it mattersTopsoil takes centuries to build but can wash or blow away in a few storms.
Both diagrams are adapted from Marshak (2019), as printed on the images. Topsoil formation rates vary widely with climate and parent rock; a common rule of thumb is hundreds of years for an inch of topsoil. This is why soil is treated as a non-renewable resource on human time scales, which sets up the soil loss section.
11Big idea 1 · Plates
Big idea 1 · tectonic plates and landscapes

Moving plates build landscapes

Movement of tectonic plates in Earth's lithosphere produces earthquakes, volcanoes, mountains and lowlands.

Landscapes are made of landforms (surface features like mesas, canyons and valleys) and topography (the shape of the land).

Humans have been changing Earth's landscape to meet our needs, sometimes with unexpected negative consequences.

Three kinds of plate boundary · tap a card to see the landforms it makes

The continents once fit together as Pangaea and have drifted apart over about 300 million years. Plates move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Arizona's own landscape comes from these forces: the Basin and Range country around Phoenix formed as the crust stretched and broke into blocks. Diagram adapted from Marshak (2019).
11Big idea 1 · Rock cycle
Big idea 1 · energy flows, materials cycle

The rock cycle

The rock cycle creates, breaks, moves and recycles rocks over time. It is how Earth recycles and renews its surface.

Step through the cycle with the buttons. The sun and Earth's internal heat supply the energy; the rock material itself is recycled, which is the rule from Module 2: energy flows, materials cycle. Local examples: the granite in Camelback Mountain and the sedimentary layers of the Grand Canyon. The cycle is not a fixed loop: any rock type can be weathered, melted or metamorphosed.
11Big idea 1 · Erosion
Big idea 1 · breaking down and moving

Weathering and erosion

  1. Once exposed at the surface, rocks are subject to weathering: physical and chemical processes that reduce rock to smaller particles and alter minerals.
  2. Rocks break into gravel, sand or silt, depending on their original minerals.
  3. Every day, wind, water, ice and gravity pick up and move these particles. That movement is erosion.
Physical weathering

Rock splits without changing: freeze and thaw, roots, desert heating and cooling.

Chemical weathering

Minerals change: rainwater dissolves limestone, iron rusts red.

The Grand Canyon is Arizona's textbook case: the Colorado River cut down through the rising Colorado Plateau and carried the sediment away. Remember the rule: energy flows, materials cycle. Weathered particles don't disappear; they become sediment and, eventually, new rock or new soil. Diagram adapted from Marshak (2019).
11Big idea 1 · Rivers
Big idea 1 · processes that shape land

Rivers move land from mountains to the sea

A watershed collects rain and sends it, with its sediment, down one river system.

Human activity can affect sediment movement:

  • Dams trap sediment in reservoirs
  • Levees keep floods, and new mud, off floodplains
  • Plowed fields and construction add sediment
The Mississippi drains about 40% of the lower 48 states. Its delta is shrinking partly because dams and levees keep sediment from reaching it. Closer to home, Glen Canyon Dam traps most of the Colorado River's sediment in Lake Powell, so the river below runs clearer and beaches in the Grand Canyon erode. 11Big idea 1 · Quick question
Quick question · the Mississippi River

Where does the Mississippi begin and end?

What is a watershed?

Think, tell a neighbor, then tap to check.

All the land that drains its rain and snowmelt into one river, lake or sea. The Mississippi's watershed reaches from the Rockies to the Appalachians and covers about 40% of the lower 48 states.

What is a delta?

Think, tell a neighbor, then tap to check.

The landform a river builds where it slows down at its mouth and drops its sediment, splitting into many channels. The Mississippi's bird's-foot delta in Louisiana is built from mud carried all the way from that watershed.

A quick check after the rivers slide, not recorded. The Mississippi starts as a small stream flowing out of Lake Itasca in northern Minnesota and ends about 2,300 miles later in the Gulf of Mexico below New Orleans. Ask for the two definitions out loud before tapping the cards. Link back: dams and levees keep sediment from reaching the delta, so the delta is shrinking.
11Big idea 2 · Soil loss
Big idea 2 · human impacts on Earth's surface

Agriculture and soil loss

1 · Adapt or mitigate

Earth processes like plate tectonics and erosion are inevitable. Humans can adapt to them or mitigate their impacts.

2 · We can degrade land

Soil degradation and erosion can be a side effect of farming: bare, tilled fields; one crop year after year; overgrazing.

3 · We can restore it

Degraded landscapes can be rehabilitated or restored after human use.

The Dust Bowl of the 1930s is the classic US case: plowing up the southern Great Plains grasslands, then drought, let the wind strip the topsoil. It led Congress to create the Soil Conservation Service in 1935 (now the Natural Resources Conservation Service). Monoculture drains the same nutrients every year and leaves soil bare between crops. 11Big idea 2 · Mining
Big idea 2 · human impacts on Earth's surface

Mining and reclamation

Mining is not considered sustainable, but abandoned mine sites can be reclaimed and rehabilitated to improve their ecological value.

Import topsoil

Rebuild a layer plants can root in.

Plant trees

Roots hold the soil; shade and leaves rebuild it.

Careful grazing

Sustainable livestock grazing keeps grass cover.

Mines in Mongolia, the European Union and China have been successfully rehabilitated.

Arizona produces most of the copper mined in the United States, so this is a local issue: open pits, tailings piles and acid drainage that carries metals into streams. Reclamation cannot put the removed rock back, but it can stabilize slopes, cover tailings and restore plant cover. Ask: who should pay for reclamation, the company, the state, or buyers of copper?

Land use change and urbanization

Photo: The Republic

  1. Overland flow happens in a rainstorm when soil is saturated, or when rain falls too fast for the soil to absorb it.
  2. It also happens on paved surfaces that are impervious: water cannot pass through them, so it becomes runoff.
  3. More pavement sends more storm water flowing as runoff, and less recharges the ground.

11Big idea 3 · Regeneration
Big idea 3 · repairing the ground

Restoration and regeneration

  • A holistic view of agriculture: ecological, social, hydrological, agronomic and economic
  • Let land rest to rejuvenate, as in rotational farming
  • Practice what nature teaches us: rely on renewable energy and organic matter
  • Integrative practices: cover crops, no-till farming, windbreaks and terraces
Each pictured practice keeps the soil covered or slows water and wind: terraces slow water on slopes, no-till leaves last season's residue as a mulch, windbreaks cut wind speed, and cover crops keep roots in the ground between harvests. The takeaway

The ground is a living, moving system. How we farm, mine and build decides whether it keeps supporting life.

  1. Plates, the rock cycle, weathering and erosion build and renew Earth's surface and soil.
  2. Soil loss, mining and urbanization change both the structure and the functions of ecosystems.
  3. Regenerative practices keep soil covered, rested and alive, so it can recover.
