How have our food systems changed?

1 · Land
~40%

Together, croplands and pastures are now the largest land biome, covering almost 40% of Earth's land surface.

A biome is a large region defined by its climate and the life in it, like desert or forest.

2 · The system

A food system is the complex web of processes that connect agricultural production to food consumption.

From the field to the factory, the truck, the store and the plate, many people and many decisions are linked together.

3 · Fewer crops
60%

The types of crops we eat have decreased tremendously.

Industrial agriculture

In the US, government farm policy pushed farms toward monoculture: growing one crop over a large area.

Policy 1

Production efficiency through monoculture

One crop at large scale is cheaper to plant and harvest by machine.

Food production and sustainability

Food is an ecosystem service

It is a vital benefit provided by "nature". It relies on net primary production by copying the processes of less designed and managed ecosystems.Net primary production: the energy plants store as new growth, after using some to live.

Food sufficiency

Having enough food has been the goal from foraging societies to modern civilizations.

The Green Revolution

It increased production yield and volume. But it needs massive external inputs and has externalities.Externality: a cost that falls on others, like polluted water, and is not in the price of the food.

2nd law of thermodynamicsEvery time energy changes form or moves, some turns into heat that cannot do work again. So no step is 100% efficient.

14Big idea 3 · Lessons
Big idea 3 · more food, new costs

Modern agricultural practices: The Green Revolution

1

New technologies

High-yield varieties of wheat, rice and corn, developed in the 20th century, led to the "Green Revolution".

2

Yields tripled fast

After 1960, average grain yields tripled to 6 metric tons per hectare in only 40 years.

3

Machines and fuel

Gas and diesel plows and harvesters replaced human and animal power. This saved time and effort, but meant more farm equipment and more fossil fuel use.

4

Thirsty crops

A huge demand for reliable water led to large dams, reservoirs and irrigation systems, which affect the water supply.

Big idea 3 · health, ecology, fairness and care

Regenerative agriculture

Source: agricaptureco2.eu

1Minimize soil disturbance

Plow and dig less, so soil life and soil carbon stay in place.

2Maximize crop diversity

Grow many kinds of plants, not just one crop.

3Keep the soil covered

Plants or mulch protect bare soil from sun, wind and rain.

4Keep living roots year-round

Roots feed soil life and hold the soil together.

5Integrate livestock

Animals graze and add manure in a planned way.

6Understand your farm's context

Each farm has its own climate, soil, people and goals.

The takeaway

Our food system reaches from the farm to the plate, and every step has costs we can change.

  1. Industrial farming and the Green Revolution raised yields, but with heavy use of fuel, water and chemicals.
  2. Energy is lost at each step up the food chain, so what we eat changes how much land and water we need.
  3. Solutions such as regenerative farming, less food waste and local food systems can make food fairer and healthier.
