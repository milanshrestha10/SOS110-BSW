15Big idea 1 · Energy
Big idea 1 · start with the definition

What is energy?

Definition

Energy is power that is derived from the utilization of physical or chemical resources.

Everything humans do needs energy. There is no sunlight, no civilization, no food and no life without it. Energy flows through the Earth system.

Primary sources · they exist in nature
Biomass Solar Wind Fossil fuels Nuclear Hydroelectricity
Secondary sources · energy carriers made by people
Electricity Gasoline

People transform primary sources into these carriers so energy can be moved and used.

Quick question: is electricity a primary or a secondary source?

Tap to check.

Secondary. People make it from primary sources such as coal, natural gas, wind, sunlight or flowing water.

Big idea 1 · energy through human history

Energy in human civilization

For nearly all of human history, our energy was our own muscle and fire. Energy changes line up with big human events and inventions.

Quick question: when did the first coal-fired steam engine appear on the clock?

Look at the top of the clock, then tap to check.

11:57:45 PM, the year 1698. That leaves only about two minutes before midnight, and the first oil well (1859), nuclear fission (1942) and solar panels (1955) all come after it.

Quick question: what powered people for most of the day?

Tap to check.

Our own internal energy, and fire from burning biomass. Animals for work and sails came later, and waterwheels and windmills later still.

Big idea 1 · seven things to know about energy

Energy principles

1Energy is a physical quantity that follows precise natural laws.
2Physical processes on Earth are the result of energy flow through the Earth system.
3Biological processes depend on energy flow through the Earth system.
4Many sources can power human activities, and the energy often must be moved from source to destination.
5Energy decisions are shaped by economic, political, environmental and social factors.
6The amount of energy a society uses depends on many factors.
7The quality of life of people and societies is affected by energy choices.

Source: US Department of Energy, "Energy Literacy" (2017)

15Big idea 1 · Energy
Big idea 1 · air, health and climate

Less burning, healthier air

The problemBurning fossil fuels for electricity makes air pollution, including fine particles, ozone, sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. These can cause breathing illness. It is also the largest source of US greenhouse gas emissions from human activity (EPA, 2017, cited in the reading).
The benefitEfficiency and renewable energy replace fossil-fuel generation, so there is less pollution to breathe and fewer warming gases.

Quick question: who gains first when a nearby power plant runs less?

Tap to think.

People who live near the plant breathe less of its pollution. Pollution can also travel far, so people downwind gain too.

One example from the reading · 2013 · US power sector
43,900metric tons less nitrogen oxides (NOx)
4,800metric tons less fine particles (PM2.5)

Cuts credited to state renewable portfolio standards (NREL, 2016).

Source: US EPA (2018), section 1.2.2.

15Big idea 2 · Fossil fuels
Big idea 2 · where fossil fuels come from

From swamp to steam

Hundreds of millions of years agoPlants in swamps die and are buried or submerged, so they do not decay.
100 million years agoDead plant matter is buried under sediment and put under heat and pressure.
TodayOver millions of years, the plant matter is transformed into coal.

Quick question: why is coal called non-renewable?

Tap to check.

It takes hundreds of millions of years to form, far longer than the few centuries we have spent burning it.

An early steam engine. Coal-fired steam engines began the shift from muscle, wind and water to fossil fuels (1698).

Big idea 2 · why we depend on fossil fuels

The current energy dilemma

The world cannot work without energy, and fossil fuels still supply about 79% of US energy. Tap each reason we depend on them to see what it costs.

Why we depend on themThe cost
Why it matters for humanityPollution harms health, and digging and drilling can disrupt ecosystems and displace communities.
Why it matters for sustainabilityCO₂ and CH₄ emissions drive climate change, and we are using up a supply that future generations will need.
Why it is hard to switchRoads, pipelines, power plants and vehicles are built around fossil fuels, so change is slow.
15Big idea 2 · Fossil fuels
Big idea 2 · what does a source give back?

Energy return on investment

Energy spent: 10
Energy you get: 100
10:1For every 1 unit of energy spent, you get 10 back.

Teaching numbers. Real EROI values depend on the source and how it is measured.

EROIThe usable energy a source delivers divided by the energy spent to get and refine it.
Why it mattersA high EROI means a big net energy gain. It helps us judge whether a new source is worth building.

Quick question: can a source with an EROI below 1 power a city?

Move the sliders, then tap to check.

No. It uses more energy than it delivers, so it works like a battery or a sink, not a source. A battery is a secondary energy carrier: it moves energy made somewhere else.

15Big idea 3 · FEW nexus
Big idea 3 · systems thinking in the energy sector

Feedback loops

Balancing loop

Produces stability. It corrects itself.

A thermostat compares the room to a target. The bigger the gap, the harder the heater works, so the gap shrinks.

In energy: a higher price makes people use less, which lowers the price again.

Reinforcing loop

Leads to instability. It grows on itself.

More births make more people, and more people make more births. The growth speeds up, like a snowball.

In energy: cheap energy helps the economy grow, and a bigger economy asks for more energy.

Teaching numbers, not real data. Look at the shape of each line.

L15The FEWs nexus and energy systems
Energy transition

Energy transition is a much-needed step for humanity to mitigate climate risks & impacts.

  1. Fossil fuels powered modern life, but they cost us pollution, resources and a changing climate.
  2. Judge any energy source by what it gives back (EROI), not only by what it costs.
  3. Think in loops and links, and watch for trade-offs, even with renewable choices.
