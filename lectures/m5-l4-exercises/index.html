5.4Exercise 4 of 5 · Leverage points
Class exercise · discuss with a neighbor

Leverage points for a resilient Phoenix

Shallow: change a number

Quick to do and easy to see, but the system keeps behaving the same way.

More trucks · bigger warehouses · more gardens · an ad campaign · a plea

Deep: change how the system works

Slower to build, but it changes the information, rules or goals that steer everyone's choices.

A shared dashboard · contracts with many regions · zoning and buying rules · a donate-or-compost rule · a new goal

Talk it over firstWhich stage of the food system would your leverage point change: grow, process, transport, store and sell, or eat and waste? Who would need to agree to it?

Run the game on the previous slide to see how your picks did.

Idea from Donella Meadows, "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System" (1999). The model's effect sizes are made-up teaching numbers.

5.4Exercise 5 of 5 · Choose a case
Class exercise · work with a neighbor · about 15 minutes

Follow a cascading risk

Cascading riskA chain reaction where a failure or disruption in one system causes a series of failures in connected systems.
Causal loop diagramA map of links between things. A + link moves both the same way. A − link moves them opposite ways. Loops are R (reinforcing) or B (balancing).

Pick one case with your neighbor. Then build its causal loop diagram, one link at a time.

Option A

Chesapeake Bay dead zone

Farms, cities and air pollution add nutrients to the rivers. Algae bloom, die and use up the oxygen. Crabs, oysters and fish pay the price.

Nutrients → algae → low oxygen → dead zone

Option B

Juneau glacial outburst flood

Meltwater fills a basin held back by glacier ice, then drains in days into the Mendenhall River. Floods damage homes, and gauges and forecasts give warning.

Melt → full basin → outburst flood → damage, and early warning helps people get out
