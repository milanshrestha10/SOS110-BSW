    1.2Environmental science
    S&M Chapter 1

    What is environmental science?

    The study of how the natural world works, and how people and the environment affect each other.

    Natural sciences

    Biology, chemistry, geology and climate science explain how ecosystems, water and air work.

    Social sciences

    Economics, policy and ethics explain why people make the choices they do.

    Sustainability

    Uses that evidence to meet today's needs without harming future generations.

    Observe→Ask a question→Hypothesize→Test with data→Share and revise
    Stress that environmental science is interdisciplinary. It is also different from environmentalism: science describes what is happening and why, while ethics and values (next slides) ask what we should do about it. Sustainability needs both. The scientific process at the bottom is the one S&M use in Chapter 1.
    1.2Valuing nature
    Environmental ethics

    Two ways to value nature

    Intrinsic value

    Worth protecting for its own sake

    Living things and ecosystems have value simply because they exist.

    "A saguaro deserves to live whether or not anyone ever sees it."

    Utilitarian value

    Worth protecting because it helps us

    Nature is valued for what it gives people: food, water, timber, money.

    "Desert tourism brings visitors and jobs to Arizona."

    Most real decisions mix both. Look back at the warm-up poll: which answer used which kind of value?

    Answer the warm-up: A is utilitarian, B is intrinsic. Ask: which argument is easier to put in a budget? Which one protects species that have no known use? Utilitarian arguments are persuasive in economic decisions but can leave "useless" species unprotected; intrinsic arguments cover them but are harder to weigh against costs.
    1.2Sorting game
    Game · intrinsic or utilitarian?

    Sort the reasons

    Intrinsic: it matters for its own sake. Utilitarian: it matters because it is useful to people.

    Bees pollinate crops worth billions of dollars.
    A rare desert pupfish has a right to exist, even with no use to us.
    A mountain lake nobody visits is still worth protecting.
    Keep the forest because its timber can be sold.
    Wetlands save cities money by cleaning water.
    We should not drive any species extinct.
    Old-growth trees deserve respect for their age alone.
    The Grand Canyon brings tourism dollars to Arizona.
    Give pairs three minutes, then check. Good follow-up: take one intrinsic reason and rewrite it as a utilitarian one (for example, the pupfish could be valued for research or as an indicator of spring health). This shows the same thing can be defended with either kind of value. The desert pupfish is an endangered fish native to Arizona and California.
    1.2Resilience
    How systems respond to pressure

    Resilience and tipping points

    High resilience

    A deep valley: a shock (a storm, a dry year) pushes the ball, but it rolls back. The system recovers.

    Low resilience

    Years of pollution make the valley shallow. Now a small push tips the system into a new state that is hard to leave.

    This is the "ball and cup" picture used by ecologists. Resilience is the depth of the valley: how big a shock the system can absorb and still recover. A tipping point is the ridge. Slow pressure (like fertilizer building up) does not change the lake much at first, it just makes the valley shallower, so the change looks sudden when it comes. Other examples to mention: coral reefs turning into algae-covered reefs, and grasslands turning into shrubland. Source for the shallow-lake example: Scheffer et al. (2001), "Catastrophic shifts in ecosystems", Nature 413.
    1.2Simulation
    Simulation · the Shallow Lake

    When does your lake tip?

    1. Set your water plants. Then raise the runoff slowly, one step at a time.
    2. Note the level where your lake turns murky.
    3. Now cut runoff back down. When does it clear again?

    A teaching model, not a real lake. Every lake has a slightly different tipping point.

    Water clarity
    Clearplants, fish, swimming
    0 out of 100 · not tipped yet
    Pairs run the lake twice: once with few water plants and once with many. More plants means more resilience, so the lake tips later. The key moment is the way back: cutting runoff to the level where it tipped does not clear the lake; it has to go much lower. The line on the graph traces a loop, not a single line. In real shallow lakes, algae shade out the plants, and without plants the mud stirs up and releases more nutrients: a feedback that keeps the lake murky. Ask: if you managed this lake, what would you do before it tips? The admin page shows where each student's lake tipped and whether it recovered.
    1.2Data analysis
    Data · Mauna Loa, Hawaiʻi

    How fast is carbon dioxide rising?

    Approximate yearly averages in parts per million (ppm), rounded. The axis starts at 300 ppm.

    Your turn

    Pick two years. How much did CO₂ rise each year, on average?

    to

    Compare the 1960s with the 2010s. Is the rise steady, or speeding up?

    Source: NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, Mauna Loa CO₂ annual mean record (Keeling curve, measurements started in 1958 by Charles David Keeling). Values are rounded annual means: 1960 about 317, 1970 about 326, 1980 about 339, 1990 about 354, 2000 about 370, 2010 about 390, 2020 about 414 ppm. Pre-industrial levels were about 280 ppm. The average yearly rise was a little under 1 ppm per year in the 1960s and about 2.4 ppm per year in the 2010s. Link to tipping points: scientists watch long records like this because many climate tipping points (for example ice sheets) respond to slow, steady pressure. Link to the scientific process: this is the "test with data" step.
    1.2Ethics discussion
    Think · pair · share

    Should the town pay to save the lake?

    A small farm town's lake has tipped and turned murky. Restoring it means paying farmers to cut fertilizer and replanting the shore for many years.

    Utilitarian view

    Clean water brings back fishing, tourism and safe drinking water. Do the benefits cover the cost?

    Intrinsic view

    The fish, birds and plants of the lake matter for their own sake, whatever it costs.

    Resilience view

    Prevention is cheaper than recovery. Should we protect nearby lakes before they tip?

    1 minuteThink alone: which view is closest to yours?
    3 minutesPair: who should pay, the farmers, the town or taxpayers?
    3 minutesShare: one argument your partner made that you had not considered.
    This is the environmental ethics objective in practice. Push students to name which kind of value their argument uses. Good probes: farmers' livelihoods are also part of the triple bottom line (people and profit). Who is harmed by the murky lake but did not cause it? That question previews externalities, which is Tuesday's topic.
    1.2Exit ticket
    Exit ticket · counts for participation

    Farms cut fertilizer back to earlier levels, but the lake stays murky. Which idea best explains this?

    Correct answer: B. A describes the opposite of what happened. C mixes up a value with a process. D contradicts the simulation. If many students pick A, revisit the valley picture: the lake is now resting in the "murky" valley, and it is resilient there too.
