3.1Watch
A country in stage 5

Why China's population is shrinking

Video: Vox, "Why China's population is shrinking" (YouTube).

  • Fewer birthsAbout 9 million babies were born in China in 2023, roughly half as many as in 2016.
  • More deaths than birthsIn 2022 China's population fell for the first time in about 60 years.
  • Fertility far below 2.1Women now have about 1 child each on average. A population needs about 2.1 to replace itself.
  • An older countryFewer young workers will support more retirees in the coming decades.
Play the video (about 7 minutes). Ask students to watch for the four numbers on the right. China moved through the whole transition in about 70 years, much faster than Europe did in 150 to 200 years; the one-child policy (1980 to 2015) came after fertility had already fallen from about 6 to under 3 in the 1970s. Figures are rounded from China's National Bureau of Statistics and the UN; please check them against the video before class.
3.1The model
Demographic Transition Model

Deaths fall first, births follow later

Birth rate (CBR)Babies born each year per 1,000 people.
Death rate (CDR)Deaths each year per 1,000 people.
Natural increaseBirth rate minus death rate. 20 per 1,000 is 2% growth a year.
Total fertility (TFR)Children per woman over her life. About 2.1 replaces the parents.
Life expectancyYears a newborn can expect to live at today's death rates.

After Max Roser (2019), "Demographic transition: Why is rapid population growth a temporary phenomenon?", Our World in Data. Curves are a sketch, not data.

Walk through the stages left to right. Stage 1: high births and high deaths, little growth (Sweden before 1800). Stage 2: better food, clean water and medicine cut deaths, but families stay large, so the population booms (Niger today). Stage 3: as child deaths fall, schooling spreads and people move to cities, families get smaller (Kenya). Stage 4: both rates low, little growth (United States). Stage 5: deaths exceed births (Japan, China). Roser's key point: if fertility had fallen at the same time as mortality, there would have been no population boom at all; the boom is the gap between the two lines.

Common surprises: in Niger, health spending alone makes the population explode (stage 2), because families stay large until schooling, jobs and family planning catch up. In Kenya, pushing hard on everything overshoots below replacement. In Japan and China, only family support, immigration and a later retirement age help. The "Cut clinic budget" card is the trap: more children die, so parents have more births, and the country fails the health checks. Each finished game is saved to the admin page.
      3.1Debrief
      Talk with your neighbor

      What did your country teach you?

      1. Why did the population grow fastest just after deaths fell?
      2. Which cards lowered fertility, and why did they take a generation to work?
      3. Why is a shrinking, aging country a sustainability problem too?
      Takeaway: rapid population growth is a temporary gap between falling deaths and falling births. Health, schooling for girls and choice over family size close that gap, not higher death rates.
      Ask two or three students from different countries to share their star count and one decision. Link back to carrying capacity and the ecological footprint: population size matters, but so does how much each person consumes, which is where the rest of this module goes.
