1.4Key vocabulary
Glossary · Module 1 · tap a card for its example

Key vocabulary

In a SETs diagram, the parts are boxes and the effects are arrows. A feedback loop is a path of arrows that leads back to where it started.

Flip both cards. The glossary calls a speeding-up loop "positive feedback" and a slowing-down loop "negative feedback". In this session we use the clearer words reinforcing (speeds up the change) and balancing (pushes back against the change), because students often read "positive" as "good". The ice melting example on the back of the card is a reinforcing loop, and it appears in the loop check game.
1.4How to draw it
Four steps

How to draw a SETs diagram

Ecological

Nature: water, soil, air, climate, plants, animals. Rivers, heat, fish, forests.

Social

People and their choices: groups, jobs, rules, money. Farmers, residents, laws, prices.

Technological

Things people build and use. Dams, canals, pumps, cars, air conditioners.

  1. List the parts of your system and tag each one ecological, social or technological. Aim for at least two of each.
  2. Draw arrows from a cause to what it affects. Write on each arrow what flows or what changes, for example "irrigation water" or "pumping lowers the water table".
  3. Mark each arrow + or −. + means the two parts move the same way: more snow, more river water. − means they move in opposite ways: more pumping, a lower water table.
  4. Find a feedback loop: follow the arrows until you get back to where you started. Then say if the loop is reinforcing or balancing (next slide).
Stress step 2: an arrow with no words is the most common mistake on the Review Exercise. Some parts are hard to tag (is a farm social or technological?). Tell students there is often more than one fair answer; what matters is that they can explain their choice. Tip: if a part is a group of people or a rule, call it social; if people built it, call it technological.
1.4Signs and loops
Reading + and − around a loop

Two kinds of feedback loop

Reinforcing: the change keeps growing

Two − links (an even number), so the loop is reinforcing. Melting speeds up more melting.

Balancing: the change gets pushed back

One − link (an odd number), so the loop is balancing. A bigger catch pulls the fish stock back down.

Count the − links around the loop. Even (0, 2, 4…) means reinforcing. Odd (1, 3…) means balancing.
Walk the reinforcing loop out loud: warmer Arctic, so less sea ice (opposite way, −); less ice, so the ocean absorbs more heat (opposite way, −); more heat, so a warmer Arctic (same way, +). Two minus signs cancel, so the loop feeds itself. The balancing loop comes from Tuesday's fishery simulation: more fish lets boats catch more (+), and more catch leaves fewer fish (−). Also mention that a reinforcing loop can run downhill too (fewer fish, fewer young, even fewer fish).
1.4Loop check
Game · count the − links

Reinforcing or balancing?

Each loop ends where it started. The sign after each arrow tells you if the next part goes the same way (+) or the opposite way (−).

Warmer Arctic → − sea ice → − heat absorbed by the ocean → + warmer Arctic
Rabbits → + foxes → − rabbits
People in a city → + jobs → + people moving in
Room temperature → + air conditioner running → − room temperature
Traffic → + wider roads built → + easier driving → + traffic
Groundwater pumping → − water table → − pumping cost → − groundwater pumping
Give pairs 3 minutes. Answers: reinforcing, balancing, reinforcing, balancing, reinforcing, balancing. Debrief the last row carefully because it has three minus signs: more pumping lowers the water table (−); a lower water table makes pumping cost more (−, because deeper wells need more energy); higher cost means less pumping (−). Three is odd, so balancing. The traffic row is called "induced demand": wider roads often fill up again.
1.4Worked example
Worked example · Sonoran Desert

Water in the Salt River Valley

Highlighted loop · balancing

Pumping lowers the groundwater (−). Falling groundwater led to stricter water rules (−). Rules limit pumping (−). Three − links is odd, so this loop is balancing.

Ecological 5 parts
Technological 4 parts
Social 3 parts
Trace the water from top left: snow and rain feed the Salt, Verde and Colorado rivers; SRP dams on the Salt and Verde (Theodore Roosevelt Dam was finished in 1911) store it; SRP canals and the 336-mile Central Arizona Project canal (Colorado River water) deliver it to farms and cities. Below the dams the Salt River through Phoenix is dry most of the year, which is why riparian (streamside) habitat gets a − arrow. The highlighted balancing loop is real history: falling groundwater levels led to Arizona's 1980 Groundwater Management Act, which set up Active Management Areas (including Phoenix) that limit pumping. Click "Show the other loop" for the reinforcing heat loop: hotter days mean more air conditioning, and air conditioners push waste heat outside; research in Phoenix (Salamanca and others, 2014) found this can warm nights by more than 1 °C in some places. Ask: which arrows cross from ecological to social or technological and back?

    The practice rubric

    2. Does at least one arrow go from nature to people or technology, and another come back to nature?

    1.4Exit ticket
    Exit ticket · counts for participation

    Reservoir level → − water price → − water use → − reservoir level. What kind of loop is this?

    Correct answer: B. There are three − links, an odd number, so the loop is balancing: when the reservoir drops, the city raises the price, people use less, and the reservoir recovers. Some cities really do use drought pricing like this. If time allows, ask which parts are ecological, social and technological (reservoir level: ecological; price and water use: social; the reservoir itself is built, so some students may call it technological, which is fine if they explain it).
